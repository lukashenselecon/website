Development Economics. The course opens with the facts about development, poverty and inequality, then works through the basic theories of growth and poverty traps before turning to the areas where most of the empirical work happens: education, migration, labour markets, health, credit and firms, agriculture, poverty alleviation programmes, environment and climate, and gender. Behavioural economics comes in where individual decision-making matters. Running alongside is the econometric toolkit development economists actually use — randomised controlled trials, difference-in-differences, regression discontinuity — applied in Stata. Undergraduate and graduate students take the course together and are graded differently; graduate students also draft and present a research proposal. There are no formal prerequisites, though econometrics and microeconomic theory help.

Research Seminar. For students in the Future Leaders international undergraduate programme, and built to prepare you for your undergraduate thesis. It runs as a mix of short lectures and student presentations: turning an interest into a research question, finding your way around academic databases and existing literature, collecting and analysing data, and writing, citing and presenting the result. Most sessions are discussion rather than lecture, and the final presentation carries half the grade.

Syllabi, readings, and problem sets are distributed through Peking University’s course platform, which enrolled students can access directly.

Office hours: Tuesdays 10:30–11:45, Guanghua Building 2, Office 343. Email ahead if you want to discuss something specific — otherwise just come by.

Supervision

I supervise Bachelor, Master, and PhD theses, and I am happy to work in English throughout — you do not need to write in Chinese.

Most of my students work on applied microeconomics, very broadly defined, with a strong empirical component. That is a wider tent than my own research. Recent theses have looked at how provincial management of local courts shapes labour disputes and firm survival, how trade unions affect the distribution of corporate tax cut windfalls, the fiscal impact of expanding Medicare coverage of GLP-1 drugs, firm-level evidence from the World Bank Enterprise Surveys, and the renminbi’s prospects as a global reserve currency. If your question can be taken to data, it is probably in scope.

What I look for is a question you care about and a credible way of answering it — not a topic close to my own papers. If you are not sure whether your idea is far enough along, that is usually a good moment to come and talk rather than a reason to wait.

How to approach me: email me with a rough idea of what you would like to work on. It does not need to be a formal proposal — a paragraph saying what question interests you, why, and what data you imagine using is plenty.

PhD students. If you are considering writing your PhD with me, write to arrange a one-on-one meeting. A doctorate is a long commitment on both sides and is worth an hour of conversation rather than an exchange of emails.

Write to lukas.hensel@gsm.pku.edu.cn. If you need a letter of reference, see the Reference letters page.
